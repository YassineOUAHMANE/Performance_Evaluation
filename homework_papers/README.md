- **What are its weak points?**

## The template I used

| Section | Why I use it |
|---------|--------------|
| **Central idea** | To say in a few lines what the article is about and what its goal is. |
| **Three important takeaways** | To keep only the main points and not get lost in details. |
| **Interesting observations** | To note things I noticed while reading (good or strange). |
| **Patterns identified** | To see how the article is organized (problem -> method -> results). |
| **Practical applications** | To see where the idea can be used in real life. |
| **Critical thinking** | To question the article: its assumptions, limits, and whether I agree. |
| **My feedback on the article** | To give my final opinion: worth reading, structure, relevance, weak points. |

## Why this template
- **Same structure for every article:**  it will be easy to compare them.
- **It goes from understanding to judging:** first I explain what the article says,
  then I check if it is good.
- **It makes me read critically:** A nice title or a known publisher is not enough;
  I must check that the content, the data and the results are really there.
- **It is short:** Each section has a few lines, so the review stays clear and to the point.

## Why I went beyond the professor's questions
The professor asks three questions: Is the paper worth reading? Is it famous? Any other point?
To answer them well, I first need to understand the paper. So the first sections help me
understand it, and the last section, **My feedback on the article**, gives my answers.
Also, a famous paper is not always a good one, so I checked the content and the weak points too.
